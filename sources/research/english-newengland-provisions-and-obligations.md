# Scope, method and conventions

## What this brief covers

This brief sets out what English law and custom, and the law of Massachusetts Bay, Plymouth, New Hampshire and Vermont, required of kin toward one another: who governed the household, who inherited, what a widow could claim, what an aged parent could demand from the son who took the farm, who had to maintain whom under the poor laws, who took charge of orphans and servants, and who had to consent to a marriage. It closes with a list of "candidate duties" written as role → role obligations for use in The Way.

# 1. Headship of the household and "family government"

## England: the householder and the hired household

English law treated the household as a unit under one head: the husband and father, who was also master of any servants living "within the walls". Blackstone builds his law of persons on three "great relations in private life" (master and servant, husband and wife, parent and child), plus guardian and ward (Blackstone, *Commentaries* 1.14–1.17).

- **Husband.** By coverture "the husband and wife are one person in law", and the wife is under "his wing, protection, and cover" (Comm. 1.15, p. 430). By the old law the husband could give his wife "moderate correction", "in the same moderation that a man is allowed to correct his servants or children". This power "began to be doubted" under Charles II, and a wife could by then have security of the peace against her husband (Comm. 1.15, pp. 432–433).
- **Father.** He may "lawfully correct his child, being under age, in a reasonable manner". His power over the child's person "ceases at the age of twenty one", though he may appoint a guardian by will. He holds his son's estate only "as his trustee or guardian". He may take "the benefit of his children's labour while they live with him, and are maintained by him", as he would from apprentices or servants. A mother "as such, is entitled to no power, but only to reverence and respect" (Comm. 1.16, p. 441).
- **Servants as members of the household.** A general hiring of a menial servant is construed as a hiring for a year, "that the servant shall serve, and the master maintain him, throughout all the revolutions of the respective seasons; as well when there is work to be done, as when there is not". Under 5 Eliz. c. 4, "All single men between twelve years old and sixty, and married ones under thirty years of age, and all single women between twelve and forty, not having any visible livelihood, are compellable by two justices to go out to service" (Comm. 1.14, p. 413). This compulsion to enter a household is the closest English counterpart to the New England laws below. Hiring and service for a year, or apprenticeship, also gave a settlement in the parish where one last served forty days (Comm. 1.14, p. 415).
- **What social historians add.** Richard Smith, summarising Cambridge Group work, stresses English "neolocalism": married couples set up their own households, so that "different generations had a high degree of residential independence", while support for the old could still come from children nearby or at a distance ("intimacy at a distance") (R. Smith, "How did the elderly poor survive?", CamPop blog, 17 Apr. 2025). This is the core of the Laslett/Cambridge Group picture of the English nuclear household. I checked it only through Smith's post, not through Laslett's own books.

## New England: family government as a public duty

Massachusetts and Plymouth turned the English expectation that everyone lives under a household head into enforceable law, administered by town selectmen.

- **Children and Youth (Massachusetts, 1642).** The selectmen are to watch that no family suffers "so much Barbarism" as not to teach children and apprentices to read English and know the capital laws. "All Masters of families" must catechise "their children and servants" weekly, and all parents and masters must bring up children "in some honest lawfull Calling, Labour or imployment". Where families remain negligent, the selectmen, with two magistrates or the county court, "shall take such children or apprentices from them, and place them with some Masters for yeares, (boyes till they come to twenty one, and girles eighteen yeares of age compleat)" (*General Laws and Liberties*, 1672 rev., tit. Children and Youth, s. 1 [1642], p. 26).
- **Disobedience.** Disobedient children and servants may be whipped (not over ten stripes) by order of one magistrate (ibid., s. 2, p. 27). A child over sixteen who curses or smites a natural parent faces death, unless the parents were "very unchristianly negligent" or provoked the child by "extreme and cruel Correction". The "stubborn or rebellious son" provision (Deut. 21) is also among the capital laws (ibid., Capital Laws 13–14, p. 15). These capital provisions are recorded here for completeness. They are not candidate duties.
- **Single persons.** "All Towns shall take care from time to time, to Order and Dispose all single persons and in mates within their Towns, to service or otherwise" (ibid., tit. Townships, s. 3, p. 148).
- **The 1668 order.** A General Court order, printed as a broadside in 1668 and reprinted in the 1672 code, complained that the law was neglected. It required constables to list "all those young persons within the bounds of your Town, who do live from under family Government, viz. do not serve their Parents, or Masters, as Children, Apprentices, hired Servants, or journey men, ought to do, and usually did in our Native Country; being subject to their Commands and Discipline" (Massachusetts broadside 1668, Evans-TCP N29421; reprinted in the 1672 code, pp. 149–150). The phrase "as ... usually did in our Native Country" is the colonists' own statement that this was English practice made compulsory.
- **Tithingmen (Massachusetts, 1675–1677).** The selectmen were to choose "sober and discreet persons", each of whom "shall take the charge of ten or twelve Familyes of his Neighbour-hood, and shall diligently inspect them" (order of 15 Oct. 1675). A later law (c. 1677) required tithingmen to be chosen annually and to "present the names of all single persons that live from under Family Government stubborn & disorderly Children & Servants", and others guilty of "omission of Family Government, nurture and religious dutyes and instruction of Children and Servants" (*Sundry Laws ... Wherein the Duty of Tything Men is expressed*, Boston, 1677?, Evans-TCP N00177, pp. 1–3). **[uncertain]** The exact enactment date of the annual-choice clause; the broadside's last dated item is 10 Oct. 1677.
- **Plymouth.** No one may become a householder or build a dwelling without allowance. "No single person that is of evil conversation ... be suffered to keep such house or live alone; And if upon due warning, such person do not put himself into some well Governed Family", a magistrate or the selectmen may "put him to service" (*General Laws and Liberties of New-Plimouth*, rev. 1671, ch. IX, s. 2, p. 29). Selectmen must list "all single persons that live from under Family Government" (ch. VIII, Mispending of Time, p. 27). Plymouth's education law follows the Massachusetts pattern: fines of 10s, then 20s, then removal of children "boyes till they come to twenty one, and Girls eighteen" (ch. VIII, Education of Children, pp. 26–27).

## The household head's duties in the conduct books

The "family government" laws assumed a theory of reciprocal duties, which the devotional literature set out at length:

- *The Whole Duty of Man* (1658) lists the duties of children to parents, of parents to children, of brethren, and of husband, wife, master and servant (Partitions XIV–XV, pp. 278–329).
- Cobbett divides filial honor into honor "of Respect, ... of Reverence, ... of Obedience, ... of Recompence" (*A Fruitfull and Usefull Discourse*, 1656, ch. I, p. 4; chs. III–VI).
- Willard's lectures on Shorter Catechism Q. 64 treat the same relations in turn: parent and child, husband and wife, master and servant, aged and young (*Compleat Body of Divinity*, 1726, Q. 64, Sermons CLXXV–CLXXXVIII, pp. 598–647).

The 1662 Prayer Book catechism gives the lay summary: "My duty towards my Neighbour is ... To love, honour, and succour my father and mother: To honour and obey the King ... To submit myself to all my governors, teachers, spiritual pastors and masters ... and to do my duty in that state of life, unto which it shall please God to call me" (BCP 1662, Catechism). The word *succour* carries the material duty discussed in section 5.

# 2. Inheritance

## English common law: the canons of descent and primogeniture

Blackstone's canons of descent for land held in fee simple (Comm. 2.14, pp. 208–217):

1. Inheritances "shall lineally descend to the issue of the person last actually seised, in infinitum; but shall never lineally ascend" (p. 208).
2. "The male issue shall be admitted before the female" (p. 212).
3. "Where there are two or more males in equal degree, the eldest only shall inherit; but the females all together" (p. 214). This is primogeniture among sons, coparcenary among daughters.
4. Lineal descendants "shall represent their ancestor" (p. 217).

Later canons deal with collaterals. Pollock and Maitland trace primogeniture's spread from military fees to socage and, in part, to villein land. Villein tenements commonly went to one heir, but "either the eldest or the youngest seems usually to have succeeded to his father to the exclusion of his brothers" (Pollock & Maitland, *HEL* ii, 2nd ed. 1898, ch. VI §2, pp. 278–279).

## Partible and other customs: gavelkind, borough-English, manorial customs

- **Gavelkind (Kent).** Blackstone's list of its properties (Comm. 2.6, pp. 84–85):
  - the tenant may alienate at fifteen;
  - the land does not escheat on the tenant's execution for felony ("the father to the bough, the son to the plough");
  - the tenant could devise by will before the Statute of Wills;
  - "The lands descend, not to the eldest, youngest, or any one son only, but to all the sons together".

  Pollock and Maitland add that within the partible custom "the hearth (*astre*) is reserved for" the youngest son, and that the widow's free bench was lost "by unchastity" (HEL ii, ch. VI §1, pp. 271–272, citing Statutes of the Realm i, 224). They also caution that "It is only to modern eyes that the inheritance partible among sons is the main feature of gavelkind" (p. 272).
- **Borough-English (ultimogeniture).** Blackstone calls it the custom by which "the youngest son, and not the eldest, succeeds to the burgage tenement" (Comm. 2.6, p. 83). Pollock and Maitland say the name is "a mere accident", taken from a case of 1327 about the two boroughs of Nottingham, and that "the true home of ultimogeniture is the villein tenement; among villein tenements it has widely prevailed" (HEL ii, ch. VI §2, pp. 279–280).
- **Regional patterns.** Goody, Thirsk and Thompson (eds.), *Family and Inheritance: Rural Society in Western Europe 1200–1800* (Cambridge, 1976) contains, among others, Cicely Howell on peasant inheritance customs in the Midlands, 1280–1700, Margaret Spufford on inheritance customs and land distribution in Cambridgeshire, 16th–18th centuries, Joan Thirsk on "The European debate on customs of inheritance, 1500–1700", and E. P. Thompson's "The grid of inheritance". I confirmed the chapter list, not the chapters' arguments.

  R. J. Faith, "Peasant families and inheritance customs in medieval England", *Agricultural History Review* 14 (1966), is the standard short survey (bibliographic details only checked).

  George Homans, *English Villagers of the Thirteenth Century* (1941), has separate chapters on partible and impartible inheritance and on "The Family in Champion Country". A *Journal of British Studies* article on Chiltern custom describes one of Homans's impartible forms as keeping a single heir while the holder kept control during his lifetime (known to me only from that secondary description).
- **Abolition.** The Administration of Estates Act 1925, s. 45(1)(a), abolished, for deaths after 1925, "All existing modes rules and canons of descent ... whether operating by the general law or by the custom of gavelkind or borough english or by any other custom of any county, locality, or manor".

## Personal property, the "reasonable part", and the Statute of Distributions

- **Medieval rule.** Goods were divided in thirds: one to the heirs or lineal descendants, one to the wife, one at the testator's disposal. "The shares of the wife and children was called their reasonable part" (*pars rationabilis*), recoverable by writ *de rationabili parte bonorum* (Comm. 2.32, p. 492). Blackstone says this survived in the city of London, the province of York and Scotland. There, if a man left widow and children, "his substance (deducting the widow's apparel and furniture of her bed-chamber, which in London is called the widow's chamber) is divided into three parts; one of which belongs to the widow, another to the children, and the third to the administrator" (Comm. 2.32, p. 518).
- **Statute of Distributions (22 & 23 Car. II c. 10).** On intestacy: "One third shall go to the widow of the intestate, and the residue in equal proportions to his children, or, if dead, to their representatives". With no issue, half goes to the widow and half to the next of kin (Comm. 2.32, pp. 515–516). A child advanced in the father's lifetime must bring the advancement "into hotchpot" (Comm. 2.32, pp. 517, 519).
- **Freedom of testation.** Outside these customary areas, English law left a man free to disinherit by will. Blackstone contrasts the civil law's *querela inofficiosi testamenti*: "Our law has made no provision to prevent the disinheriting of children by will" (Comm. 1.16, p. 437). Macfarlane's *Origins of English Individualism* (1978) builds a wider argument on this testamentary freedom. I did not consult it.

## New England: partible inheritance with a double portion to the eldest son

New England rejected primogeniture for intestate estates in favor of division among all children, with a "double portion" (two shares) to the eldest son. Orth notes the biblical model of Deut. 21:17 (J. V. Orth, "After the Revolution: 'Reform' of the Law of Inheritance", *Law and History Review* 10 (1992), 33–44; only the opening and footnote list checked).

- **Massachusetts Body of Liberties (1641).** No. 81: "When parents dye intestate, the Elder sonne shall have a doble portion of his whole estate reall and personall, unlesse the Generall Court upon just cause alleadged shall judge otherwise". No. 82: with no sons, "their Daughters shall inherit as Copartners" (Body of Liberties, nos. 81–82, Old South Leaflets vol. 7, pp. 271–272).
- **Massachusetts code (1641, 1649).** The county court may assign the widow "such a part of his estate as they shall judge just and equal", and divide the rest among children, "Provided, the Eldest Son shall have a double Portion, and where there are no Sons, the Daughters shall inherit as Copartners, unless the Court upon just Cause alledged shall otherwise determine" (*General Laws and Liberties*, 1672 rev., tit. Wills, s. 3 [1641, 49], p. 158).
- **Plymouth.** When a man dies intestate leaving several children, "the Eldest Son shall have a double Portion with what he hath already received from him of his Estate, both real and personal", the rest inheriting "as Copartners" unless the court orders otherwise (*General Laws of New-Plimouth*, ch. X, s. 6, pp. 36–37).
- **Massachusetts Province (1692).** The intestacy act explains why land should be divided among children: "estates in these plantations do consist chiefly of lands which have been subdued and brought to improvement by the industry and labour of the proprietors, with the assistance of their children, the younger children generally having been longest and most serviceable unto their parents in that behalf, who have not personal estates to give out unto them in portions" (Province Laws 1692–93, ch. 14, preamble, *Acts and Resolves* i, pp. 43–44). Its terms:
  - the widow takes one third of the personal estate "forever, besides her dower or thirds in the houses and lands during life";
  - the residue, real and personal, goes "by equal portions" to the children, with advancements brought into account, "except the eldest son then surviving ... who shall have two shares, or a double portion of the whole";
  - where land "cannot be divided among all the children, without great prejudice to or spoyling of the whole", the judge may give it all to the eldest son (or, on refusal, another son), who must pay the others their shares (ibid., pp. 44–45).
- **New Hampshire Province (1718).** An act in almost identical words: one third of the personalty to the widow plus her dower, the rest equally to the children, "except the Eldest Son then Surviving ... Who shall have Two Shares, or a double Portion of the whole", with the same power to settle an indivisible farm on one son (Act for the Settlement and Distribution of the Estates of Intestates, 14 May 1718, *Laws of New Hampshire* ii, ch. 28, pp. 295–296). Batchellor notes an antecedent act of 1693, disallowed by the Crown in 1706.
- **Vermont (1779).** No double portion. The 1779 act gives the widow one third of the personalty "besides her dower, or thirds", and the rest "by equal portions, to and among the children", with advancements equalised, "and the same shall be so divided, as that the male heirs shall have their parts in the real estate, so far as the estate will allow; and where there are no sons, the daughters shall inherit as co-parceners" (An Act for the settlement of testate and intestate estates, Laws passed February 1779, *Vermont State Papers*, pp. 339–342). The preamble says intestate land had, "by ancient and immemorial custom, and common consent of the people", "descended to and among the children".
- **Abolition of the double portion.**
  - Massachusetts: the 1783 statute (St. 1783, c. 36, s. 1) still gave "the eldest son then surviving ... two shares". "This exception was abrogated by St. 1789, c. 2", effective 1 Jan. 1790, after which "all children took in equal shares, without regard to sex or primogeniture" (*Miller v. Miller*, 51 Mass. 393 (1845), per Shaw C.J., as reported online).
  - New Hampshire: an act of 3 Feb. 1789 repealed the 1718 act and provided that undevised land "shall descend in equal shares among his children and such as legally represent such of them as may be dead". Personalty not bequeathed is "distributed in the manner real Estates descend" (*Laws of New Hampshire* v, pp. 384–385).
- **Practice.** Greven's study of Andover (*Four Generations*, 1970) is summarised by others as showing that, as land grew scarce, fathers kept control of land late and increasingly settled the farm on one son to keep it viable. His chapter is titled "Control and Autonomy: Families and the Transmission of Land" (known to me only from secondary summaries and a table of contents).

  Ditz, *Property and Kinship: Inheritance in Early Connecticut, 1750–1820* (Princeton, 1986), compares four upland towns with a Connecticut River town. Per the publisher's description, late-colonial customs "strongly preferred certain male heirs" and created jointly held property rights; this persisted upland, while the commercial river town moved toward more egalitarian and individual arrangements. The pattern of sons taking land and daughters taking movables is widely reported for New England. I did not verify it page by page. **[uncertain as to degree]**

# 3. Provision for widows

## England: dower, quarantine and free bench

- **Dower at common law.** Where the husband was seised of an estate of inheritance, the widow "shall have the third part of all the lands and tenements whereof he was seised during the coverture, to hold to herself for the term of her natural life" (Comm. 2.8, p. 129). Pollock and Maitland add that she was entitled to "a dower house" but not to the capital messuage (HEL ii, p. 422).
- **Quarantine: the right to stay in the house.** Under Magna Carta, "nothing shall remain in her husband's capital mansion-house for forty days after his death, during which time her dower shall be assigned. These forty days are called the widow's quarentine" (Comm. 2.8, p. 135). Pollock and Maitland put it thus: "During these forty days the widow had a right ... to remain in the principal house and to be maintained at the cost of the as yet undivided property". To "assign" dower within the forty days "was the duty of the heir or of his guardian" (HEL ii, ch. VII §2, pp. 421–422).
- **Remarriage.** Magna Carta also provided that a widow "shall pay nothing for her marriage, nor shall be distreined to marry afresh, if she chooses to live without a husband" (Comm. 2.8, p. 135).
- **Free bench and gavelkind dower.** In gavelkind, "the widow became entitled to a conditional estate in one half of the lands, with a proviso that she remained chaste and unmarried; as is usual also in copyhold dowers, or free-bench" (Comm. 2.8, p. 129). Copyholds were "not liable to dower" at common law (p. 132), so the copyhold widow depended on the manorial custom of free bench. Pollock and Maitland: the surviving spouse in Kent "enjoys, so long as he or she remains single, one-half of the land of the dead spouse. This right ... bears the name of 'free bench'". Outside Kent the term usually means the widow's right. A "less determinate right to remain at the fireside and enjoy a 'free bench'" gave way in time to fixed shares, and the gavelkind widow's free bench "shows its ancient origin when she has to abandon it on a second marriage" (HEL ii, pp. 418–419, 423–424).
- **Curtesy.** A husband who had issue born alive capable of inheriting held his deceased wife's inheritance for life as "tenant by the curtesy of England" (Comm. 2.8, p. 126).
- **Erosion and abolition.** Jointures settled before marriage could bar dower; the Massachusetts law below reflects the same rule. The Dower Act 1833 (3 & 4 Will. IV c. 105) let a husband defeat dower by any absolute disposition by deed or will, so that the widow took dower mainly where he died intestate as to the land (as described in a 19th-c. real-property treatise reproduced online; statute text not checked). The Administration of Estates Act 1925, s. 45(1)(b)–(c), abolished curtesy and "Dower and freebench" for deaths after 1925.

## New England: thirds by statute, and relief against unjust wills

- **Massachusetts dower law.** Because "no provision hath been made for any certain maintenance of Wives after the decease of their Husbands", every married woman not "estated by way of joynture" before marriage shall have "by way of Dowry ... one third part of all such Houses, Lands, Tenements and Hereditaments, as her Husband was seized of ... at any time during the Marriage, to have and enjoy for the tearm of her natural life". This holds free of the husband's debts and conveyances unless she consented in writing before a magistrate. If the heir does not set out "her just third part" within a month of demand, the court will assign it "by Mets and Bounds". She must not commit "strip or waste" and must keep the houses and fences in repair (*General Laws and Liberties*, 1672 rev., tit. Dowries, pp. 42–43). **[uncertain]** The bracketed date printed is [1641], but the text refers to conveyances before "the last of November 1647", and the law is usually dated 1647.
- **Body of Liberties no. 79.** "If any man at his death shall not leave his wife a competent portion of his estaite, upon just complaint made to the Generall Court she shall be relieved." No. 80 frees a married woman "from bodilie correction or stripes by her husband", except in self-defense (Body of Liberties, nos. 79–80, p. 271).
- **Plymouth.** On intestacy the widow takes "one third part of the Rents and Profits of his Lands during her life, and a third of Goods and Chattels, Debts being first paid, at her dispose". If a man "make an irrational and unrighteous will, whereby he deprives his Wife of her reasonable allowance for her subsistency", the court may relieve her out of the estate. This applies "especially ... where the Wife brought with her good part of the Estate in Marriage, or hath by her diligence and industry done her part in the getting the Estate" (*General Laws of New-Plimouth*, ch. X, ss. 4–5, p. 36). This is a distinctive Plymouth recognition of the wife's labor as a ground of claim.
- **Province and states.** The 1692 Massachusetts, 1718 New Hampshire and 1779 Vermont intestacy acts each give the widow one third of the personalty absolutely, "besides her dower or thirds" in the land for life (above).

  Dower survived in Massachusetts as a statutory life estate in one third of land owned at death (G.L. c. 189, s. 1, as in force 2006). Chapter 189 was repealed by St. 2008, c. 521, and dower and curtesy were abolished by G.L. c. 190B, s. 2-112 (from online statute compilations).

  For widows' practical position, Holly Bentley Mitchell's thesis on 136 Portsmouth, NH widows (1816–1826) concludes that widows had "a carefully defined place in society" but that their financial security was usually secondary to creditors' claims, and their control of real estate often limited to a life interest ("'Power of thirds': the material lives of widows, Portsmouth, New Hampshire, 1816–1826", MA thesis, University of Delaware, 1991; abstract only).

## The widow's right to remain in the house

English common law gave the widow forty days' quarantine in the capital messuage, then a dower third. It did not give her the principal house itself (above).

In New England the right to a room or part of the house was secured mostly by private instrument (wills, deeds and bonds) rather than by general statute. Pilgrim-era Plymouth wills reportedly give the widow "a convenient room" in a son's house, or the use of the dwelling house for life (as quoted in a search excerpt of the Pilgrim Hall transcriptions of the wills of Phineas Pratt and John Howland, 1702). I could not open the transcriptions themselves. **[uncertain: wording not verified]**

# 4. Maintenance agreements between aging parents and the inheriting child

## Medieval England: retirement contracts in the manor court

Elaine Clark's article is the anchor here: "Some Aspects of Social Security in Medieval England", *Journal of Family History* 7:4 (1982), 307–320. Its abstract says medieval historians "have long maintained that social welfare in the communities of rural England often involved private systems of support for the elderly", that "individually arranged pension plans provide a case in point", and that "the best evidence of these pension plans is found in the records of manor courts". A library record describes them as negotiated bargains in which "the conditional transfer of property was meant to guarantee security during retirement". I read only the abstract and metadata.

The general pattern in this literature is that an aged tenant surrenders the holding in court to a son, a son-in-law or even a non-kinsman. In return the incoming tenant binds himself, on record, to provide specified food, fuel, clothing and a room or separate cottage, and the surrender is void if he fails. I know this pattern from secondary descriptions; I could not open a sample court-roll entry here. **[uncertain as to specific clauses]**

Homans's chapters on impartible inheritance are the classic early treatment (secondary description only). Richard M. ## New England: maintenance deeds and bonds

New England sources show the same exchange, written as a deed to the son plus a bond in which he promises maintenance.

- **Dublin, New Hampshire (1824).** Solomon Piper, aged 69, conveyed a one-half undivided interest in the farm to his youngest son Artemas for a recited $800. Artemas gave his father a bond in the same sum, conditioned on his:
  - carrying on "the whole of said farm and manage the same agreeably to the rules of good husbandry during the natural life of the said Solomon";
  - delivering yearly "one half of all the produce of every kind";
  - keeping four cows and seven sheep for his father;
  - paying half the taxes;
  - doing "all necessary nursing that the said Solomon and Susanna, wife of the said Solomon, may stand in need of";
  - bringing "all necessary fire wood suitable cut for the fire".

  The bond made no provision for Susanna if she outlived Solomon. Solomon's will provided for her instead.
- **Dublin, New Hampshire (1844).** Samuel Moore deeded the whole farm to his son James for a recited $1,000. James's bond in the same sum bound him:
  - "to support and maintain the said Samuel Moore and his present wife Abigail Moore in sickness and health during their lives or the life of the survivor";
  - to pay his father's debts and poll taxes;
  - to pay them "five dollars in money" yearly;
  - "To furnish them with comfortable house room and fire wood prepared for their fire places";
  - to provide "suitable meals and drinks", "good wearing apparel suitable for their age and station in life", "a horse and carriage", and "doctoring and nursing";
  - "To cause them at all times to be treated with due respect" and "To entertain their friends when they may wish to visit them";
  - and, should they be "deprived of reason", to make the provision "to the satisfaction of the selectmen of the town".

  (Both bonds: Dublin Historical Society [NH], *Newsletter* no. 60, Feb. 2004, "The Way We Lived Then: Eldercare Before RiverMead", signed J.W.H., quoting originals in the Society's archives.)
- **Maine.** A Biddeford record of 1828 is described as a son's acknowledgment of receiving his father's farm and personal property, "pledging in return to take care of his parents" (Maine Memory Network item contributed by the Biddeford Historical Society; seen only as a search excerpt). Craig's study of northern Maine farm transmission (*History of the Family* 10:3, 2005) is said to find one child taking the farm in exchange for old-age support (abstract-level only).
- **Vermont.** Reading notes on Barron's *Those Who Stayed Behind* (Cambridge, 1984), a study of Chelsea, Vermont, summarise the 19th-century pattern as "older sons leave, younger sons inherit farm and parents" (University of Vermont course reading notes, 2005). This is a third-hand summary. **[uncertain]**

Legally these were private contracts. A son who failed to perform forfeited the bond, which allowed the parent to sue or, under some deeds, to re-enter. The Moore bond's appeal to the selectmen shows that the town, as ultimate poor-law guarantor, had a stake in their performance.

# 5. The legal duty of kin to support one another; settlement and warning out

## England: 43 Eliz. c. 2 and its interpretation

- **The relatives' clause.** "And be it further enacted, That the Father and Grandfather, and the Mother and Grandmother, and the Children of every poor, old, blind, lame, and impotent Person or other poor Person not able to work, being of a sufficient Ability, shall, at their own Charges, relieve and maintain every such poor Person in that Manner, and according to that Rate, as by the Justices of Peace of that County where such sufficient Persons dwell, or the greater Number of them, at their General Quarter Sessions shall be assessed; upon Pain that every one them shall forfeit twenty Shillings for every Month, which they shall fail therein" (Poor Relief Act 1601, 43 Eliz. c. 2, s. VII, in the *Statutes at Large* numbering as transcribed at workhouses.org.uk). **[uncertain]** Section numbering differs between editions; in this text s. VI is the appeal proviso.
- **Other duties in the Act.** Section I makes overseers set to work "the Children of all such whose Parents shall not ... be thought able to keep and maintain their Children" and raise money for "the necessary Relief of the Lame, Impotent, Old, Blind". Section V lets them bind poor children apprentice "till such Man-child shall come to the Age of four and twenty Years, and such Woman-child to the Age of one and twenty Years, or the Time of her Marriage".
- **Blackstone's gloss.** "It is a principle of law, that there is an obligation on every man to provide for those descended from his loins", met by the 1601 Act's provision that parents and grandparents of "poor impotent persons shall maintain them at their own charges, if of sufficient ability". A deserting parent's goods may be seized for the children (5 Geo. I c. 8). A stepfather is liable for a stepchild where the mother was able before remarriage, but only while she lives (Comm. 1.16, pp. 436–437). The duty runs only to children "impotent and unable to work", and only for "necessaries", the penalty being "no more than 20 s. a month" (p. 437).

  The duty also runs upward: children owe "protection in the infirmity of their age" to parents. Unlike Athenian law, English law does not dissolve the tie for parental misconduct, so a child "is equally compellable, if of sufficient ability, to maintain and provide for a wicked and unnatural progenitor, as for one who has shewn the greatest tenderness" (Comm. 1.16, pp. 441–442).
- **The duty's limits.**
  - Lineal only: brothers, sisters, uncles and in-laws are not named. Massachusetts courts later read the parallel statute strictly; see *Farr v. Flood* below.
  - Conditional on "sufficient ability".
  - Enforced by a fixed money penalty assessed by the justices.
- **Enforcement in practice.** Richard Smith writes that "there is limited evidence that this was enforced" (CamPop blog, 2025). David Thomson's article on the 19th century ("'I am not my father's keeper': families and the elderly in nineteenth century England", *Law and History Review* 2 (1984), 265–286) is reported to conclude that magistrates did not really expect the children of the poor to support their parents, and that the Poor Law's real thrust was against neglectful husbands and fathers. I saw only a repository record attributing that conclusion and could not read the article. **[uncertain]**
- **End of the duty.** The National Assistance Act 1948, s. 42, restated liability to maintain as "a man shall be liable to maintain his wife and his children" and "a woman shall be liable to maintain her husband and her children". Grandparents and adult children are not named. The Elizabethan duty of children to parents is not continued in that section (my reading of s. 42 as enacted; I did not trace the repeal schedule).

## England: settlement and removal (1662)

Within forty days of a newcomer coming "to settle ... in any Tenement under the yearly Value of ten Pounds", two justices may remove anyone "likely to be chargeable to the Parish" to the parish "where he or they were last legally settled, either as a Native, Housholder, Sojourner, Apprentice or Servant, for the Space of forty Days at the least", unless security is given. Appeal lies to quarter sessions (13 & 14 Car. II c. 12, ss. I–II). Later statutes and case law made hiring for a year a common way to gain a settlement (Comm. 1.14, p. 415). The family relevance: a wife took her husband's settlement and children their father's, so the parish of the household head determined where the whole family could claim relief.

## Massachusetts and Plymouth: town responsibility and warning out

- **Colonial code.** The shire court, or two magistrates, may "dispose of all unsettled persons into such Towns as they shall judge to be most fit". Anyone resident in a town more than three months "without notice given ... that the Town is not willing that they should remain as an Inhabitant amongst them" must, in case of necessity, "be provided for, and relieved ... by the Inhabitants of the said place" (*General Laws and Liberties*, 1672 rev., tit. Poor, p. 123). The colonial code has no relatives'-liability clause.
- **Plymouth.**
  - "Every Town in this Government shall maintain their own poor".
  - If relieved families do not employ their children "towards the getting of a livelihood", or any family "cannot or doth not provide competently for their Children", the selectmen may "place out such Children into good Families" (ch. IX, s. 4, p. 29).
  - A newcomer entertained three months and not warned becomes the town's charge (s. 5).
  - Children or adults sent to another town to be nursed, schooled or cured remain the charge of the sending town (s. 6).
  - A master who brings in an apprentice or covenant servant who falls sick or lame must maintain him "by his Masters particular charge, during the date of his Indentures", after which the town relieves him (s. 3, p. 29).
- **Province of Massachusetts (1692).** The relatives' duty arrives in New England law here. A newcomer entertained three months without being warned becomes "the proper charge" of the town if in need, "unless the relations of such poor impotent person in the line or degree of father or grandfather, mother or grandmother, children or grandchildren be of sufficient ability; then such relations respectively shall relieve such poor person in such manner as the justices of the peace in that county where such sufficient persons dwell shall assess, on pain that every one failing therein shall forfeit twenty shillings for every month's neglect" (Province Laws 1692–93, ch. 28, s. 9, *Acts and Resolves* i, pp. 67–68). Note the addition of **grandchildren** to the Elizabethan list.
- **Later Massachusetts law.**
  - In 1700 the warning period was lengthened to twelve months. The Settlement Act of 1793 (Laws 1793, ch. 34) repealed the earlier settlement laws and ended warning out in Massachusetts (Benton, *Warning Out*, pp. 52–53, 114–115).
  - The kindred clause was re-enacted in 1788 and 1793 (*Town of Plymouth v. Hey*, 285 Mass. 357 (1934), which held that "by consanguinity" excludes illegitimate children).
  - Case-law excerpts cite St. 1793, c. 59, s. 3, covering kindred "in the line or degree of father or grandfather, mother or grandmother, children or grandchildren, by consanguinity", and describe it as based on 43 Eliz. (*Gleason v. Boston*, 144 Mass. 25; *Burrill v. Sermini*). *Farr v. Flood* (1853) is reported to hold that a relative by affinity is not liable. **[uncertain: these three cases seen only as search excerpts; the chapter number 59 not checked against the session laws]**
  - The duty was later codified in G.L. c. 117, s. 6 (cited in *Plymouth v. Hey*).

## New Hampshire

- **1719.** "An Act for Regulating Townships" (passed 2 May 1719) copies the Massachusetts clause. A newcomer entertained three months without warning is the town's charge, "unless the Relations of Such Poor Impotent persons in the line of Father or Grandfather, mother or Grand mother, Children or Grand children be of Sufficient ability, then Such Relations respectively Shall releive Such poor person ... on pain that every one failing therein. Shall forfeit Thirty Shillings for every months neglect" (*Laws of New Hampshire* ii, ch. 12, pp. 343–344). Batchellor notes it was amended in 1743 and repealed on 20 June 1792.
- **1787.** "An Act to enable three Justices of the peace unus quorum to determine all disputes concerning the maintainance of the poor" (19 June 1787) gives a summary remedy, with double costs, where "any relative of any poor person, in the line of father, or grandfather, mother, or grandmother, children or grand children (such relations being of sufficient ability)" disobeys the justices' order "concerning the past or future maintainance" of a poor person (*Laws of New Hampshire* v, ch. 1, pp. 251–253).
- **Warning out.** Benton says it was practised from the 1638 settlement. Three months was fixed in 1679 and twelve months in 1771, until the Settlement Act of 1796 (Benton, pp. 114–115). His chronology table gives 1718 as the "First Warning Out Act in New Hampshire" (p. 117). **[uncertain: internal inconsistency in Benton]**

## Vermont

- **1779.** "An Act for relieving and ordering idiots, impotent, distracted, and idle persons" (February 1779). Persons "naturally wanting of understanding", or who by "age, sickness, or otherwise, become poor and impotent", and have no estate, "shall be provided for, and supported, by such of their relations as stand in the line or degree of father or mother, grand-father or grand-mother, children or grand-children, if they are of sufficient ability to do the same". The county court fixes the "manner and proportion" and may issue quarterly execution against relations who default (*Vermont State Papers*, pp. 302–303).
- **Transient persons.** Benton prints a Vermont act letting selectmen warn any transient "like to be chargable to such town" to depart within twenty days and have him conveyed back, with no warning after a year's residence. With it came a poor act making each town "take care of, support, and maintain their own poor" unless the person "by law" is "to be provided for by some particular person or persons". Benton dates this act 1787 in his text but footnotes it to the Vermont Laws of February 1779 (Benton, pp. 106–107). **[uncertain: date]**
- **Settlement.** The 1787 act limited settlement to birth or ownership of £200 of estate or £10 yearly value. The act of 3 March 1797 defined settlement by purchase and occupation of a $100 freehold, two years' renting at $20, office-holding, and so on. An 1801 act let a warned person avoid the effect by serving a year as selectman, town clerk, constable, grand juror or lister. Warning out ended with the Settlement Act of 1817 (Benton, pp. 107–108, 115–117).

## What warning out meant for families

Warning out did not expel anyone by itself. It relieved the town of any future duty to support the person: those warned "were inhabitants of the town for all purposes except being helped if they needed help". They could vote and be taxed "for the support of others who were in need, but, when they came to be in need, they were entitled to no help from the taxes of the town" (Benton, p. 116).

Rhode Island never authorised it by statute but excluded newcomers through town councils. Herndon's *Unwelcome Americans: Living on the Margin in Early New England* (Philadelphia, 2001) recovers the stories of forty people warned out of Rhode Island towns, c. 1715–1800 (publisher and Rhode Island Historical Society descriptions only).

## The nineteenth-century shift

The Massachusetts 1821 Quincy report listed four ways towns provided for paupers:

1. letting them out "to the lowest bidder, in families at large" (the *vendue*);
2. letting them all to one contractor;
3. outdoor supplies "at their own houses";
4. "poor, or alms, houses".

By the 1840s most New England communities had moved from the vendue to town poor farms and workhouses. Bound-out poor children were kept "until 18" (girls) and "21" (boys). The same account observes that "Families were expected, and in many cases required by law, to care for relations in need", with town relief the "last resort" (Old Sturbridge Village, "Historical Background on the Poor and Poor Relief in Early 19th Century New England", n.d., quoting J. Quincy, *Report of the Committee on Pauper Laws*, 1821, p. 7).

# 6. Guardianship of orphans, apprenticeship and putting out, servants as family

## England

- **Kinds of guardian.** "Guardians by nature" are the father and, in some cases, the mother. Guardians "for nurture" are the father or mother until fourteen; failing them, the ordinary assigns someone. "Guardians in socage" arise where the minor holds land: by common law the guardianship "devolves upon his next of kin, to whom the inheritance cannot possibly descend" (for land from the father, the maternal uncle), "that there may be no temptation, nor even suspicion of temptation, for him to abuse his trust". At fourteen the minor may choose his guardian unless the father appointed one by deed or will under 12 Car. II c. 24 (Comm. 1.17, pp. 449–450).
- **Apprentices.** Apprentices are "bound for a term of years, by deed indented or indentures, to serve their masters, and be maintained and instructed by them". "Children of poor persons may be apprenticed out by the overseers, with consent of two justices, till twenty four years of age, to such persons as are thought fitting; who are also compellable to take them" (Comm. 1.14, p. 414; 43 Eliz. c. 2, s. V).
- **Service in husbandry.** Ann Kussmaul's *Servants in Husbandry in Early Modern England* (1981) is the standard study of unmarried youths living as servants in farmers' households, hired by the year (title and subject only checked). Blackstone gives the legal frame: the yearly hiring, the master's duty to maintain through the year, the compulsion of the unmarried to serve, and settlement by a year's service (Comm. 1.14, pp. 413–415).

## New England

- **Orphans.** No orphan not placed by the parents in their lifetime "shall afterwards be absolutely disposed of by any kindred, freind, Executor, Towneship, or Church, nor by themselves without the consent of some Court, wherein two Assistants at least shall be present" (Body of Liberties, no. 84). The 1646 code version adds that for marriage the approval of the selectmen or one assistant suffices, and that "the minority of women in case of marriage, shall be sixteen yeares" (*General Laws and Liberties*, tit. Children and Youth, s. 6 [1646], p. 28).

  Plymouth: no one under twenty-one may convey land or sue in person, "But any Orphan may chose their Guardians to act for them, at the age of fourteen years" (*General Laws of New-Plimouth*, ch. IV, s. 17, p. 13).
- **Debts of minors.** Credit given to anyone under twenty-one without written order from "their friends" is not recoverable (*General Laws and Liberties*, tit. Children and Youth, s. 4 [1647], pp. 27–28).
- **Putting out.** Selectmen could remove neglected children and place them with masters until 21 (boys) or 18 (girls) (Massachusetts 1642; Plymouth ch. VIII). They could also place out the children of families that could not provide for them (Plymouth ch. IX, s. 4).
- **Servants' liberties.** These protect the servant as a household member (Body of Liberties, nos. 85–88; *General Laws and Liberties*, tit. Masters, Servants, Labourers, ss. 6–9, pp. 104–105):
  - a servant fleeing "the Tiranny and crueltie" of a master may be harboured by any freeman of the town until relief is ordered;
  - no servant may be "put of for above a yeare to any other", even by the master's executors, without a court's consent;
  - a master who maims a servant "shall let them goe free";
  - servants who have served "deligentlie and faithfully ... seaven yeares, shall not be sent away emptie".
- **Servants' duties.** Servants may not "give, sell or truck" without the master's license (ibid., s. 1).
- **Plymouth sick servants.** A master must maintain a sick or lame indentured servant for the term (ch. IX, s. 3).
- **Willard on servants.** Masters are "to Correct them with Prudence and Humanity", correction being "a Duty incumbent on them, in case that need calls for it" (*Compleat Body*, Q. 64, Sermon CLXXIX, p. 615).

# 7. Portions, marriage settlements and parental consent

## Parental consent

- **England.**
  - Blackstone: the parent's consent to the marriage of a child under age "was also directed by our antient law to be obtained: but now it is absolutely necessary; for without it the contract is void" (Comm. 1.16, p. 441, citing 26 Geo. II c. 33, the Marriage Act 1753).
  - *The Whole Duty of Man*: "of all acts of disobedience, that of marrying against the consent of the Parent, is one of the highest. Children are so much the goods, the possessions of the Parent, that they cannot without a kind of theft, give away themselves without the allowance of those, that have the right in them" (Partition XIV, §15, p. 291).
  - Magna Carta's protection of the widow from forced remarriage is noted above.
- **Massachusetts.**
  - It is "against Rule to seek to draw away the affections of young Maidens ... before their Parents have given way and allowance". Whoever does so before obtaining "liberty and allowance from her Parents or Governours (or in absence of such) of the nearest Magistrate" is fined, and imprisoned on a third offense (*General Laws and Liberties*, tit. Marriage, s. 3 [1647], p. 101).
  - Banns must be published three times or posted fourteen days (s. 2 [1639]). Marriage is solemnised only by a magistrate or authorised person (s. 5 [1646], p. 102).
  - "No man shall strike his Wife, nor any Woman her Husband" (s. 1).
  - The check on parents: "If any parents shall wilfullie and unreasonably deny any childe timely or convenient mariage, or shall exercise any unnaturall severitie towards them, such childeren shall have free libertie to complaine to Authoritie for redresse" (Body of Liberties, no. 83; *General Laws and Liberties*, tit. Children and Youth, s. 5 [1641], p. 28).
- **Plymouth.** "None shall be allowed to Marry that are under the Covert of Parents, Guardians, Masters, or Overseers, without their consent and approbation". But "if a meet Marriage be orderly proposed, and any Master or Guardian, out of any sinistre end, or covetous desire, will not upon reasonable tearms suffer it to be", two magistrates may hear and determine the case (*General Laws of New-Plimouth*, ch. VIII, Marriages, s. 3, p. 28).
- **Willard.** Parents may not "put a force on their Children" in marriage, "yet their Advice and Direction is to be used, and their Consent is to be had ordinarily, except by their sordid and brutish Carriage, they necessitate their Children to seek redress from higher Powers" (*Compleat Body*, Q. 64, Sermon CLXXVI, pp. 604–605).

## Portions and settlements

- **The parental duty to portion.**
  - *The Whole Duty of Man*: parents must "put them into some course of living in the world; if God have blest the parents with wealth, according to what he hath, he must distribute to his children". It condemns parents who "defer all the provisions for them, till themselves be dead" and meanwhile "afford them not such a competency, as may enable them to live in the world" (Partition XIV, §22, p. 300).
  - Willard: it is "the Duty of Parents" to allow children "a suitable Portion of their Estates", "More particularly, when they dispose them in Marriage, that they may have something to begin upon". At death they should give by will "a due Proportion to every one of them, who have not before had it set of to them; nor ought they to shew Partiality, much less to Disinherit any, upon any light occasion" (*Compleat Body*, Q. 64, Sermon CLXXVI, p. 602).
- **Law: advancements count against the portion.**
  - England: hotchpot under the Statute of Distributions (Comm. 2.32, pp. 517, 519).
  - Massachusetts 1692, New Hampshire 1718 and Vermont 1779: children "advanced by settlement, or portions not equal to the others' shares" receive only enough to equalise. Plymouth counts what the eldest "hath already received" against his double portion (above).
- **Jointure.** A jointure settled before marriage bars dower in both England and Massachusetts (*General Laws and Liberties*, tit. Dowries: a wife "that shall not before Marriage be estated by way of joynture"; Comm. 2.32, p. 519, on a jointure barring the customary part). Lloyd Bonfield's *Marriage Settlements, 1601–1740* (Cambridge, 1983) is the standard study of the landed strict settlement (title only checked). The strict settlement was largely a gentry instrument. For rural middling and lesser families the portion paid at marriage and the will were the main instruments, in England as in New England. **[general characterisation; not verified against a specific source here]**

# 8. Children's duty to maintain parents in the duty literature

- **The Whole Duty of Man (1658).** "A fourth duty to the Parent is to assist, and minister to them in all their wants of what kind soever, whether weakness, and sickness of body, decayedness of understanding, or poverty, and lowness of estate, in all these the child is bound according to his ability, to relieve and assist them". Since every child in infancy received the same care, "whenever either of these becomes the Parents case, as sometimes by great age ... the child is to perform the same offices back again to them". Relieving parents' poverty is part of the fifth commandment (Mark 7), "and such a duty it is that no pretence can absolve, or acquit us of it". The book condemns those who, risen in the world, "think it a disparagement to them to look on their Parents that remain in a low condition" (Partition XIV, §16, pp. 291–293).
- **Cobbett (1656).** The fourth branch of filial honor is "Honour of Recompence". "If a Son do nothing for his Father and his Mother in their need, they break that Commandement". 1 Tim. 5:4 places widows who have "Children or Nephewes to provide for her" on "the Childrens duty (if such be living) or near kinsfolkes duty", not on the church (*A Fruitfull and Usefull Discourse*, ch. VI, pp. 127–129). When parents are in "wants or weaknesses", children "must be willing to suffer and lose somewhat with them, and for them, rather then forsake them" (p. 72).
- **Willard (1726).** Children owe parents "needful Succour in their Necessity", which is "very frequently so, especially with such [as] Live to Old Age, and past their Labour". It is "a due debt which they owe to them to retaliate when they are become helpless". "It is the Duty of Children, to take care of their Parents, to their Ability, that they be not a Publick Charge", for "none is to be reckoned Indigent of common Support, that hath Children or Grand-Children, that are able to relieve him". Unmarried children living at home should lay out "their labour & earnings" for their parents' comfort and "not presently, in hope of doing better for themselves, desert their helpless Parents". When they have families of their own, they remain bound "according to their Ability" (*Compleat Body*, Q. 64, Sermon CLXXVII, p. 608). Willard's "Children or Grand-Children" matches the Massachusetts 1692 statute's addition of grandchildren.
- **Wadsworth (1712).** *The Well-Ordered Family* has a section saying that children and grandchildren should relieve and comfort their aged, indigent parents and grandparents, citing 1 Tim. 5:4. The only text I could reach (archive.org scan) is too badly garbled to quote. **[uncertain: wording]**

## Main differences

1. **Inheritance by statute, not custom.** New England replaced the English mosaic (primogeniture, gavelkind, borough-English, manorial customs) with one statutory partible rule plus a double share. The 1692 preamble justified it by the children's labor in clearing the land. Vermont dropped even the double share in 1779. Massachusetts and New Hampshire dropped it in 1789.
2. **Family government enforced by the town.** English households were expected to absorb the young and unmarried. Massachusetts and Plymouth made it a legal duty, policed by selectmen, constables and tithingmen, with power to take children from negligent parents.
3. **A longer kin-support list.** New England added grandchildren to the Elizabethan list (Massachusetts 1692, New Hampshire 1719, Vermont 1779). The colonies also kept the town, not the parish, as the unit of relief. Warning out was a cruder tool than the English removal order: it disclaimed liability without necessarily expelling anyone.
4. **Courts' power to correct private ordering.** Body of Liberties 79 and 83 and Plymouth ch. X s. 5 let courts relieve a neglected widow, and children unreasonably denied marriage. English common law had no counterpart for the widow beyond dower.

## Changes into the nineteenth century

- **England.**
  - Dower was weakened (Dower Act 1833).
  - Customary descents, dower, curtesy and free bench were abolished (1925).
  - The relatives' clause survived into the 19th century but was reportedly little enforced against adult children. By 1948 it was confined to spouses and children.
- **New England.**
  - Equal partible descent arrived by 1789–90 in Massachusetts and New Hampshire.
  - Warning out ended: Massachusetts 1793, New Hampshire 1796, Vermont 1817. Formal settlement acts defined who belonged to a town.
  - The kindred-support duty was re-enacted (Massachusetts 1793) and lasted into the 20th century (G.L. c. 117, s. 6, cited 1934).
  - Pauper care moved from the vendue to poor farms (1820s–1840s).
  - Retirement by deed plus maintenance bond remained common on hill farms (Dublin, NH, 1824 and 1844). Barron's Chelsea, Vermont is reported to show younger sons staying to inherit "farm and parents" (third-hand).
  - Dower survived in Massachusetts as statutory law until the 21st century.

# 10. Uncertainties and gaps

- 43 Eliz. c. 2: section numbering differs between editions (s. VII in the *Statutes at Large* text used).
- Massachusetts Dowries law: printed date [1641] versus the 1647 reference in its text.
- Massachusetts tithingman laws: exact date of the annual-choice clause (c. 1677).
- *Gleason v. Boston*, *Burrill v. Sermini*, *Farr v. Flood* and the "St. 1793, c. 59" citation: seen only as search excerpts.
- Benton is internally inconsistent on the first New Hampshire warning-out law (1679 vs 1718) and the first Vermont act (1779 vs 1787).
- Medieval retirement contracts: I give the general pattern from secondary descriptions (Clark's abstract; Homans by description). No court-roll entry was read directly.
- Plymouth wills giving widows a room: wording not verified (source PDFs unavailable).
- Wadsworth 1712: content paraphrased from unreadable OCR; no quotation.
- Thomson 1984, Barron 1984, Greven 1970, Ditz 1986: conclusions taken from abstracts, publisher copy, reviews or reading notes, not the books themselves.
- Not consulted and not used as authority: Laslett (*The World We Have Lost*; *Household and Family in Past Time*), Macfarlane (*Origins of English Individualism*), Hanawalt (*The Ties That Bound*, 1986), Morgan (*The Puritan Family*), Demos (*A Little Commonwealth*), Salmon (*Women and the Law of Property in Early America*, 1986), Shammas, Salmon and Dahlin (*Inheritance in America*, 1987), Keyssar ("Widowhood in Eighteenth-Century Massachusetts", 1974). They are the obvious next reading for verifying sections 2–4.

# 12. Candidate duties

Each line states one obligation as role → role, with its source. "Law" marks enforceable law; "Custom" marks customary or private-instrument practice; "Teaching" marks devotional or catechetical teaching.

## Household head and members

- Household head → children and servants: teach them to read, catechise them weekly, and bring them up in an honest calling (Law: Mass. *General Laws and Liberties*, Children and Youth s. 1 [1642]; Plymouth ch. VIII).
- Household head → town: answer for the order of his household, or lose its children and servants to placement with another master (Law: ibid.).
- Single adult → community: live under some family government as child, apprentice, hired servant or journeyman, or be put to service (Law: Mass. Townships s. 3; 1668 order; Plymouth ch. IX s. 2).
- Tithingman → ten or twelve neighbouring families: inspect them and present those living outside family government or neglecting it (Law: Mass. tithingman laws 1675–77).
- Children and servants → household head: obey; disobedience is punishable by a magistrate (Law: Mass. Children and Youth s. 2).
- Husband → wife: maintain her and let her share in his goods, neither starving her nor wasting the estate (Teaching: *Whole Duty of Man* XV §14).
- Wife → household: bear her part of labor and industry (Teaching: *Whole Duty of Man* XV §14; Plymouth ch. X s. 5 treats her industry as a ground of claim).
- Husband and wife → each other: neither may strike the other (Law: Mass. Marriage s. 1; Plymouth ch. VIII; Body of Liberties 80).
- Master → servant: maintain him through the whole year of hiring, in slack seasons as well as busy ones (Law: Blackstone 1.14, p. 413).
- Master → sick or lame indentured servant: maintain him at the master's own charge for the term of the indenture (Law: Plymouth ch. IX s. 3).
- Master → maimed servant: release him from service and pay recompense (Law: Body of Liberties 87).
- Master → servant who served faithfully seven years: do not send him away empty (Law: Body of Liberties 88).
- Freeman → servant fleeing a cruel master: shelter him and notify the master and magistrate (Law: Body of Liberties 85).
- Servant → master: do not trade the master's goods without license; serve out the term (Law: Mass. Masters, Servants s. 1, s. 9).

## Parents and children

- Father and mother → minor or impotent child: provide necessaries (Law: 43 Eliz. c. 2; Blackstone 1.16, pp. 436–437).
- Grandfather and grandmother → impotent poor grandchild: maintain him if of sufficient ability, as the justices assess (Law: 43 Eliz. c. 2 s. VII; Mass. 1692 c. 28 s. 9; NH 1719 c. 12; VT 1779).
- Stepfather → stepchild: maintain the child if the mother was able before the remarriage, while she lives (Law: Blackstone 1.16, p. 437).
- Parent → child: set the child up in a course of living according to the parent's means, in life and not only at death (Teaching: *Whole Duty of Man* XIV §22).
- Parent → child at marriage: give a suitable portion to begin upon (Teaching: Willard Q. 64, Sermon CLXXVI).
- Parent → children at death: give each a due proportion not already received, without partiality or light disinheritance (Teaching: Willard Q. 64; Law, on intestacy: statutes of distribution with hotchpot or advancement).
- Parent → child seeking marriage: do not unreasonably deny a timely and convenient marriage (Law: Body of Liberties 83; Mass. Children and Youth s. 5; Plymouth ch. VIII).
- Child under age → parent: obtain consent to marry (Law: England 26 Geo. II c. 33; Teaching: *Whole Duty of Man* XIV §15; Willard: consent "ordinarily" to be had).
- Suitor → maid's parents or governors: obtain their leave before courting her (Law: Mass. Marriage s. 3 [1647]; Plymouth ch. VIII s. 3).
- Child → aged, infirm or poor parent: relieve and assist according to ability, as the parent once sustained the child (Teaching: *Whole Duty of Man* XIV §16; Cobbett ch. VI; Willard Q. 64).
- Child of sufficient ability → impotent poor parent: maintain the parent as the justices assess, even a wicked one (Law: 43 Eliz. c. 2 s. VII; Blackstone 1.16, p. 442; Mass. 1692; NH 1719; VT 1779).
- Grandchild of sufficient ability → impotent poor grandparent: maintain the grandparent as the court assesses (Law: Mass. 1692 c. 28 s. 9; NH 1719 c. 12; NH 1787 c. 1; VT 1779).
- Adult child → parents: keep them from becoming a public charge (Teaching: Willard Q. 64, Sermon CLXXVII).
- Unmarried child living at home → parents: lay out labor and earnings for their comfort, and do not desert them for one's own advancement (Teaching: Willard Q. 64; Cobbett, p. 72).
- Married child → parents: continue to care for them according to ability (Teaching: Willard Q. 64).
- Child who has risen in the world → humble parents: own them openly and do not be ashamed of them (Teaching: *Whole Duty of Man* XIV §16).
- Child → parents: honor, love and succour them (Teaching: BCP 1662 Catechism).

## Heirs, widows and the aged

- Heir → widow: assign her dower, a third of the husband's lands for life, within forty days, and let her stay in the principal house meanwhile (Law: Blackstone 2.8, pp. 129, 135; Pollock & Maitland ii).
- Heir → widow in Massachusetts: set out her third within one month of demand, or a court will assign it by metes and bounds (Law: Mass. Dowries).
- Widow in dower → heir: commit no waste and keep houses and fences in repair (Law: Mass. Dowries).
- Customary heir → widow on copyhold or gavelkind land: allow her free bench, often half the holding, while she remains chaste and unmarried (Custom: Blackstone 2.8, p. 129; Pollock & Maitland ii, 418–419).
- Husband → wife by will: leave her a competent portion, or the court may relieve her (Law: Body of Liberties 79; Plymouth ch. X s. 5).
- Administrator → widow: give her one third of the personal estate absolutely, besides her dower (Law: Statute of Distributions; Mass. 1692 c. 14; NH 1718; VT 1779).
- Son and heir → aged parents: maintain them in the house as the deed or custom provides (Custom: medieval surrender-and-maintenance agreements per Clark 1982; Dublin, NH bonds 1824, 1844).
- Son taking the farm → parents: work the farm in good husbandry, deliver the agreed share of produce, keep their stock, pay the taxes, supply firewood, food, clothing, house room, doctoring and nursing, treat them with respect, and receive their friends (Custom: Dublin, NH bonds 1824, 1844).
- Son taking the farm → surviving parent: continue maintenance for the life of the survivor if the deed so provides (Custom: Moore bond 1844; the Piper bond's omission shows why the clause mattered).

## Inheritance among siblings

- Eldest son → younger siblings (New England before 1789): take only a double portion; the rest go equally to the other children; daughters inherit as copartners when there are no sons (Law: Body of Liberties 81–82; Mass. Wills s. 3; Plymouth ch. X s. 6; Mass. 1692 c. 14; NH 1718).
- Son taking an indivisible farm → siblings: pay each their proportional share of its appraised value (Law: Mass. 1692 c. 14; NH 1718).
- All children → each other (Mass. and NH from 1789–90; VT 1779): share intestate land equally (Law: St. 1789 c. 2 per *Miller v. Miller*; NH Act of 3 Feb. 1789; VT 1779).
- Advanced child → siblings: bring advancements into account before sharing (Law: Statute of Distributions hotchpot; Mass. 1692; NH 1718; VT 1779).
- Eldest son → his father's land (England, common law): inherit alone, while daughters inherit together (Law: Blackstone 2.14, canon III).
- All sons → father's gavelkind land (Kent): inherit equally, with the youngest keeping the hearth (Custom: Blackstone 2.6, p. 85; Pollock & Maitland ii, 271).
- Youngest son → father's borough-English tenement: inherit it (Custom: Blackstone 2.6, p. 83; Pollock & Maitland ii, 279–280).
- Brothers and sisters → each other: hold united affections and extend kindness "in some degree to all that have any neerness of blood" (Teaching: *Whole Duty of Man* XV §1).

## Guardians and orphans

- Next of kin who cannot inherit → orphan holding land: act as guardian in socage until the orphan is fourteen (Law: Blackstone 1.17, pp. 449–450).
- Father → minor children: appoint a guardian by deed or will (Law: 12 Car. II c. 24, per Blackstone 1.17).
- Kindred, executor, town or church → orphan: do not dispose of an orphan without a court's consent (Law: Body of Liberties 84; Mass. Children and Youth s. 6).
- Orphan aged fourteen → self: may choose a guardian (Law: Plymouth ch. IV s. 17; Blackstone 1.17).
- Overseers or selectmen → poor children: bind them apprentice or place them in good families: in England to 24 (boys) and 21 or marriage (girls); in Massachusetts and Plymouth to 21 (boys) and 18 (girls) (Law: 43 Eliz. c. 2 s. V in the *Statutes at Large* numbering, s. IV in the corpus text; Mass. Children and Youth s. 1; Plymouth ch. VIII, ch. IX s. 4).
- Town → its settled poor: relieve them unless kin of sufficient ability are liable (Law: Mass. 1692 c. 28 s. 9; Plymouth ch. IX s. 4; VT 1779/1787).
- Sending town → child sent away to nurse or school: remain liable for the child's relief (Law: Plymouth ch. IX s. 6; Mass. 1692 c. 28 s. 9 proviso).
- Householder who receives such a child → town: stand as the town's security for the child's support (Law: Mass. 1692 c. 28 s. 9).

## Strangers and settlement

- Newcomer → town (before 1793 in Mass., 1796 in NH, 1817 in VT): may be warned out within the statutory period, after which the town owes no relief (Law: Benton, pp. 114–116).
- Parish → poor newcomer in a tenement under £10 (England, 1662): may remove him within forty days to his parish of settlement unless he gives security (Law: 13 & 14 Car. II c. 12 s. 1).
